# Who Is Edward Andrew Weinhaus?

## The Coach’s Record

**Publisher of ALABnews. Respondent in an Illinois disciplinary case. Defendant in two Maryland criminal cases. Billed as “Tommy Robinson’s US Lawyer.”**

*Revised October 12, 2026. Canonical URL: https://www.edward-andrew-weinhaus.com/*

---

The [message](https://adamjohnflorek.com/assets/edward-andrew-weinhaus-linkedin-law-graduate-message.webp) came through LinkedIn. After a greeting, it said: “I just read our news piece about you.” Beneath that was a link to ALABnews. Then one more line, “Believe me, I feel u bro,” and a signature: “Coach.”

The sender was Edward Andrew Weinhaus, a lawyer whose company publishes ALABnews. The recipient was a law graduate ALABnews had written about, and not one of his connections; the note arrived as an InMail, the kind of message LinkedIn lets members send to people outside their own network. Its subject line opened with a casual “fwiw” and cast someone else as the one who had given the graduate “the shaft.” The publisher of the piece presented himself as its subject’s ally.

Weinhaus goes by “Coach” elsewhere, too. His LinkedIn profile gave his name as “(Dr.) Edward ‘Coach’ Weinhaus, Esq.,” above a headline that read, in full, “Academic, Publisher, International Judicial Reformer, international Envoy, French Student, and Your Favorite Faculty. Also...Crypto.”

He has practiced law and helped build publishing businesses, and the record of his last few years is largely a court record. He carried a $25,000 fee order from his divorce through five more courts. Illinois’s attorney-discipline agency charged him with making false statements involving two judges, and his organizations sued the agency. He sued the school district whose staff had escorted him out of a homecoming game. In May and June of 2026, two criminal cases were filed against him in Maryland, each charging harassment.

## “Juicy Stories”

Weinhaus has worked for years in the business of turning public records into publications. In February 2026, [Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/tow_center/pink-slime-networks-are-weaponizing-foia.php), reporting on what it called “pink slime” publishers and their use of public-records requests, identified him as a lawyer for Metric Media, a network of nearly twelve hundred news sites, and as a cofounder of two of its predecessors, Blockshopper and Journatic, later known as LocalLabs. The magazine quoted him on the work: “I do not care what you’re asking for. I do not care what you’re using the information for.” And: “If it’s public, it’s yours and it’s all of ours. Let’s go get it.”

In 2023 he formed Judiciocracy, a Missouri company whose publications include ALABnews; [court papers filed in 2025](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/illinois/ilndce/1:2025cv03387/475706/57/) identify him as its chief executive. The same year he founded Children of the Court, a nonprofit that argues parental-rights cases should go to judges who have been parents or foster parents.

On November 27, 2023, ALABnews published [its piece about the law graduate](https://adamjohnflorek.com/assets/edward-andrew-weinhaus-law-graduate-attack-post-redacted.webp), under the byline “AlabNews Staff Writer.” It was illustrated with a cartoon of a beaming figure in cap and gown. No court file contains that drawing, and no records request could have produced it. Whatever documents lay behind the story, the smile was the publication’s choice.

In correspondence on June 4, 2026, Weinhaus wrote of “[likely tracking Mr. [redacted] behavior](https://adamjohnflorek.com/assets/edward-andrew-weinhaus-ardc-tracking-email.webp),” and observed that “there do seem to be a lot of [juicy stories available](https://adamjohnflorek.com/assets/edward-andrew-weinhaus-ardc-juicy-stories-email.webp).”

The next day he co-signed a [litigation-hold notice](https://adamjohnflorek.com/assets/litigation-hold-redacted.pdf) with Adam Florek, a lawyer whose letter names Weinhaus as the “Chief Legal Counsel” of Florek’s firm. Sent on behalf of Judiciocracy, the notice demanded the preservation of drafts, the identities of sources, and communications with the attorney-discipline agencies of Illinois, California and Missouri. It required written confirmation within five business days, and it asked that the matter be treated “as if a lawsuit has already been filed and litigation is ongoing.”

## “Nationwide Enforcer”

In April 2023, Judge Thaddeus Wilson of the Circuit Court of Cook County signed an agreed order settling a business lawsuit in which Weinhaus was a plaintiff. What followed is set out in a [complaint](https://www.iardc.org/File/GetPdfFile/4909-0400-9777?FileName=Complaint%3A+In+re+Edward+Andrew+Weinhaus%2C+Matter+Number%3A+2025PR00026.pdf) filed against Weinhaus in 2025 by the Illinois Attorney Registration and Disciplinary Commission, the agency that investigates and prosecutes misconduct by Illinois lawyers. According to the complaint, Weinhaus had the judge’s photograph placed on the “Team” page of Children of the Court’s website, with the words “Nationwide Enforcer” beneath the judge’s name. The biography said that “his order has created a national organization, advocating in every state,” and that “Judge Wilson is responsible for enforcing the funding of Children of the Court.” The complaint charges that the page falsely presented the judge as a member of the organization’s team, responsible for its activities and its funding.

Judge Regina Scannicchio, of the same court, had presided over matters in Weinhaus’s divorce. After the decree, his former wife asked the court to make him pay her legal fees, citing his litigiousness during the proceedings, and Judge Scannicchio ordered him to pay $25,000.

He fought the fee order, and then he sued the judge who entered it. The Illinois Appellate Court dismissed his appeal, and the Illinois Supreme Court declined to hear it. In federal court he sued Judge Scannicchio and the Illinois Judges Association, alleging violations of his rights to due process and equal protection. The suit was dismissed, and in June 2025 [the Seventh Circuit affirmed](https://cases.justia.com/federal/appellate-courts/ca7/24-2473/24-2473-2025-06-18.pdf?ts=1750266100), describing a complaint built on “conclusory labels” and “bare assertion[s] of conspiracy.” In February 2026 [the Supreme Court declined to hear the case](https://www.supremecourt.gov/search.aspx?filename=/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25-725.html). One $25,000 order had been carried through five more courts.

Judge Scannicchio appears in the commission’s complaint as well. In December 2023, ALABnews published an article under a headline calling a firm of Chicago divorce lawyers “dastardly.” According to the commission, the article concerned allegations against one of his former wife’s lawyers, and it reported that Judge Wilson had “immediately ordered an organization be started to keep judges like [Judge Regina] Scannicchio away from cases involving children.” The complaint charges that this, too, was false.

One judge signed his settlement and appeared on the organization’s “Team” page as its enforcer. The other ordered him to pay and appeared in ALABnews as an example of the judges to be kept “away from cases involving children,” and in federal court as a defendant.

Weinhaus [answered the complaint](https://www.iardc.org/File/GetPdfFile/4912-4888-4814?FileName=Answer%3A+In+re+Edward+Andrew+Weinhaus%2C+Matter+Number%3A+2025PR00026.pdf). The article, his answer says, must be read whole: “the full article needs to be included to understand the context and who the speaker is.” For the two judges, the answer proposes a different measure. Neither, it says, ever asked for a correction, and “a failure to take steps to correct any false information is a prima facie admission that the information is not a false statement of fact.” His article was to be read in full and in context. The judges’ silence was to be read as an admission.

A disciplinary complaint is a charge; a panel of the commission’s Hearing Board decides in the first instance whether it is proved. Weinhaus’s organizations also sued the commission in federal court, arguing that the charges were meant to stifle their speech. In December 2025, Judge April M. Perry [dismissed the suit](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/illinois/ilndce/1:2025cv03387/475706/57/). The rights at stake belonged to Weinhaus, not to his organizations, the judge ruled. It was “not a reasonable inference that the non-actions of third parties demonstrate bad faith,” and the claim that the charges were meant to stifle speech was “frankly implausible.” The organizations could file an amended complaint only if their counsel could cure its defects consistent with Rule 11, which holds lawyers answerable for what they sign. They later [appealed to the Seventh Circuit](https://dockets.justia.com/docket/circuit-courts/ca7/26-2265).

The Seventh Circuit had dealt with Weinhaus before. [In 2019](https://www.edward-andrew-weinhaus.com/Evidence/edward-andrew-weinhaus-seventh-circuit-sanctions.pdf) it found an appeal he had brought frivolous, granted his opponents’ motion for sanctions, and directed its clerk to send its orders to the State Bar of California.

## Homecoming

In October 2025, Weinhaus paid $6 for a ticket to the homecoming football game at Highland Park High School, north of Chicago, where two of his children were students. A student reported him to the dean of students, who asked him to leave. He refused.

The dean came back with the school’s head of security and three police officers and told Weinhaus to leave or be arrested for trespassing. A student, the dean said, was uncomfortable because he was “following her.” The dean escorted Weinhaus out and told him he could return for future events.

That is the evening as Weinhaus himself described it, in a federal lawsuit he filed with Children of the Court and Judiciocracy against the school district, the Highland Park police and several officials. A court deciding a motion to dismiss accepts the complaint’s factual allegations as true. On that footing, in June 2026, Judge Jorge L. Alonso [dismissed the case with prejudice](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/illinois/ilndce/1:2025cv14662/490858/43/). Members of the public have no constitutional right to be on school property, the judge wrote, and an escort from a game his children had already left, with an invitation to return, was an injury that was “truly minimal.” Weinhaus [appealed](https://dockets.justia.com/docket/circuit-courts/ca7/26-2556).

It was the second time in six months that a federal judge in Chicago had dismissed a suit brought by his organizations.

## “Oyyyyy Tommy Tommy”

By his own account, Weinhaus’s practice has reached across the Atlantic. In a [LinkedIn message](https://edward-coach-weinhaus.com/assets/edward-andrew-weinhaus-tommy-robinson-praise-linkedin-message.webp) to someone he had not yet met, under the subject line “Oyyyyy Tommy Tommy,” he wrote of “my representation of controversial UK free speech advocate Tommy Robinson” and linked to an [online interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RndmPlzJuXg) whose title billed him as “Tommy Robinson’s US Lawyer.”

“Tommy is often lambasted for his views!” the message continues. “And boy does that cause the meek to back away. But not the patriots!” He signed off as “Coach,” and added a postscript: “OYYYYY TOMMY TOMMY !”

Tommy Robinson is the name used by Stephen Yaxley-Lennon, the British far-right activist who cofounded the English Defence League. In 2018 a video spread of a fifteen-year-old Syrian refugee being forced to the ground by the throat on his school’s playing field in Huddersfield. Robinson answered with two Facebook videos accusing the boy of violence. The boy sued him for libel, and in 2021 [the High Court found](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Hijazi-v-Yaxley-Lennon-judgment-220721.pdf) that Robinson had failed to prove his allegations. His videos, the judge wrote, were “a deliberate effort to portray the Claimant as being, far from an innocent victim, but in fact a violent aggressor,” in language “calculated to inflame the situation.” The boy became a target of abuse; he and his family were forced from their home, and he had to abandon his education. The court awarded him £100,000 and barred Robinson from repeating the allegations.

Robinson did not comply. On October 28, 2024, having admitted ten breaches of the injunction, four of them involving his film *Silenced*, he was [committed to prison](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/HM-Solicitor-General-v-Yaxley-Lennon-Judgment.pdf) for eighteen months, less three days. Four of those months were meant to compel compliance: Robinson could have them remitted by purging his contempt, which, the judge said, would likely require at least taking *Silenced* down and trying to have it removed elsewhere. His [appeal against the committal](https://www.judiciary.uk/judgments/stephen-yaxley-lennon-v-hm-solicitor-general-and-another/) failed, as did a [challenge to his segregation in prison](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Yaxley-Lennon-v-Secretary-of-State-for-Justice.pdf).

In May 2025 he took the other course. In witness statements, Robinson said he now understood that if he disagreed with the injunction, the right course had been to appeal against it rather than to breach it. He had deleted the recordings from his X account, asked those who controlled other copies to remove them, and promised to comply in the future. The High Court [remitted the four months](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Solicitor-General-v-Stephen-Yaxley-Lennon-aka-Tommy-Robinson.pdf). “The intention behind the coercive element of the committal has been achieved,” the judge wrote, noting also that Robinson had expressed no contrition or remorse. The injunction stayed in force. On May 27, 2025, Robinson [was released](https://www.standard.co.uk/news/crime/tommy-robinson-prison-release-hmp-woodhill-beard-b1229828.html). Compliance, not endurance, had shortened his time in prison.

## “In My Sleep”

On May 18, 2026, a [criminal case](https://www.edward-andrew-weinhaus.com/assets/Edward-Coach-Weinhaus-Faces-Criminal-Charges-for-Harassing-Law-School-Graduate.pdf) was filed against Weinhaus in the District Court of Maryland in Montgomery County, charging harassment by electronic communication and harassment by a course of conduct. The court’s record marks probable cause for each charge; a charge is an accusation, not a finding of guilt. On June 4, the day he wrote about “juicy stories,” Weinhaus [wrote](https://adamjohnflorek.com/assets/edward-andrew-weinhaus-maryland-prison-boast-email.webp): “Frankly, I think I could do three years in Maryland state prison in my sleep.”

A second case followed. Filed on June 10, it lists the same two charges, for conduct on June 9, five days after he wrote that sentence, again with probable cause marked for each. A [warrant for his arrest](https://edward-coach-weinhaus.com/Maryland-Arrest-Warrant-Edward-Andrew-Weinhaus-D06CR26005694.pdf) was issued the day the case was filed. Both cases were open when their records were printed, and a record printed on September 18 listed the warrant as active.

## Awaiting Response

Weinhaus’s name now heads documents he did not write. *In re Edward Andrew Weinhaus* is the disciplinary case in Illinois; in his answer there, he counted two judges’ failure to seek a correction as “a prima facie admission.” *State of Maryland vs. Edward Andrew Weinhaus* is the caption of two criminal cases.

The message to the graduate is shorter than any of them: a greeting; “I just read our news piece about you”; a link; “Believe me, I feel u bro”; “Coach.” LinkedIn’s reminder listed it as “awaiting response.”
